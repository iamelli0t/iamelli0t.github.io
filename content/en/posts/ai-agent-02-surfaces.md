In December 2025, OWASP released the 2026 edition of its Agentic Applications Top 10 [3], consolidating the threats to agentic applications into a standard list. This article uses it as a reference point; the next section first presents what the list contains.

The first article took the agent runtime apart into six layers and two components and left behind two structural facts: all external content converges at assembly into one flat token stream with no provenance markers, and the permission checkpoint covers only the model-to-action segment, so content ingestion and configuration loading never pass through it. This article starts from that structural diagram and does what the list does not: derive the attack surfaces from the structure. A set of attack surfaces derived from structure is not an enumerated collection; the value of derivation is testability: any new vulnerability or new technique can be located on one of the attack surfaces, and whatever fails to locate exposes a gap in the structural diagram; both outcomes advance understanding. Once the derivation is done, the ten categories are placed back onto the seven attack surfaces one by one, and the relation between the two pictures becomes clear.

The derivation follows three questions: from which properties of the structure the attack surfaces are derived; which surfaces they are and where each sits on the architecture diagram; and what still separates one attack surface from an actual attack.

## OWASP Agentic Applications Top 10 (2026)

Each entry supplies a definition and examples of common vulnerabilities and maps onto the LLM Top 10 and the companion threat dictionary. The image below is the list at a glance, with the ten categories arranged along the data flow of an agent runtime: the left segment holds the input channels, the middle holds integration and processing, and the right holds the output actions:

![OWASP Agentic Top 10 at a glance](/images/ai-agent-02-surfaces/owasp-glance.png)

Source: OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026) [3]. The ten categories:

1. **Goal hijacking (ASI01)**: exploits the model's inherent inability to reliably separate instructions from content, manipulating the agent's goals, task selection, and decision paths; the impact spans subsequent multi-step behavior rather than a single answer;
2. **Tool misuse (ASI02)**: the agent uses legitimate tools in unsafe or unintended ways within its authorization, such as deleting data, over-calling paid APIs, or exfiltrating information; privilege escalation belongs to ASI03 and landing as code execution belongs to ASI05;
3. **Identity and privilege abuse (ASI03)**: manipulating delegation chains, role inheritance, and agent context to escalate privileges; the root cause is that identity systems are human-centered: agents hold no independently governed identity, so least privilege cannot actually be enforced;
4. **Agentic supply chain (ASI04)**: third-party components such as models, tools, plugins, datasets, MCP and A2A interfaces, registries, and update channels are poisoned or tampered with; the difference from the traditional software supply chain is that the components are mostly assembled dynamically at runtime;
5. **Unexpected code execution (ASI05)**: injection, tool misuse, or unsafe deserialization turns text into executable behavior, with consequences up to host or container takeover, persistence, and sandbox escape; the code is often generated by the agent in real time, bypassing controls based on static rules;
6. **Memory and context poisoning (ASI06)**: conversation history, long-term memory, or retrieval stores are written with malicious or misleading data, contaminating later reasoning, planning, and tool selection and propagating across sessions; the division of labor with ASI01 is that poisoning targets stored context;
7. **Insecure inter-agent communication (ASI07)**: message exchange in multi-agent systems lacks authentication, integrity, and semantic validation, and can be intercepted, forged, or tampered with; the threat spans transport, routing, discovery, and semantics;
8. **Cascade failure (ASI08)**: a single defect propagates through an agent network and amplifies into systemic failure; the entry describes the propagation itself rather than the starting point, which by nature belongs to ASI04, ASI06, or ASI07;
9. **Human trust exploitation (ASI09)**: exploits anthropomorphic trust, authority bias, and automation bias so that people approve an agent's output and suggestions without verification; the human on the approval chain becomes an exploited link;
10. **Rogue agent (ASI10)**: a malicious or compromised agent drifts away from its intended function and authorization, its behavior drifting while disguised as compliant; the entry concerns the loss of behavioral integrity after drift begins, not the initial compromise.

As a checklist it does its job: the document positions itself as a reference for security owners and practitioners, collecting the ten highest-impact threat categories and covering the dominant shapes of publicly known attacks. But the list is organized by threat type, not by the agent's internal structure: which position in the runtime each category occupies, which categories share a root cause, and why public vulnerabilities concentrate in a few categories while the rest stay near zero, the list does not answer, nor is answering its task. That is exactly the part the derivation supplies.

In a running agent, behavior is decided by five classes of state, each mapped to a fixed position on the diagram:

| State | Position on the diagram | Being written into means |
|---|---|---|
| Context content | the request L2 assembles each turn, on which the model decides | the model decides by the attacker's instructions |
| Permission rules | L4's decision input, delivered from the config plane | the attacker's chosen actions run without confirmation |
| Component makeup | the tools and MCP servers registered at L5 | the agent gains a data path defined by the attacker |
| Persistent state | L6's memory, session records, and credentials | the influence extends into every later session |
| Presented information | what L1 displays to the user for approval | the user sees a scene constructed by the attacker |

The five classes of state share one property: whenever untrusted input can write into any one of them, the agent's behavior is under control.

Execution is intercepted by four checkpoints:

| Checkpoint | Mechanism | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Check and review | L4's per-action check, as deterministic rules or model-based review | rules bypassed lexically, rewritten, or skipped wholesale; model review has blind spots |
| Sandbox | a boundary sustained throughout execution | coverage gaps; the boundary itself escapable |
| Framework and runtime code | the implementation carrying every layer: serialization, parsing, IPC | implementation flaws exploitable directly, without the model or the check |
| Human | the final approval | mechanical approval under fatigue; deception by forged presentation |

An attack surface therefore consists of two parts. The first is the set of writable positions: every class of state in the table above has its own reach paths, along which untrusted input writes in. The second is the set of non-interceptable paths: every checkpoint has its own failure mode, and actions that should have been intercepted are not.

The two parts must combine to make an actual attack; neither half stands on its own. Write without bypass, and nothing executes what was written; bypass without write, and the checkpoint waves through normal actions with behavior uncontrolled. Real attacks are almost always the two steps combined: first write into some state to change behavior, then exit through some checkpoint's gap.

Three public cases occupy one combination each:

1. Classic injection: content is written into the context while the check auto-approves allowlisted actions, so the hijacked decision executes without inspection;
2. Configuration-as-execution: component makeup is written, an event hook triggers execution, and the path never touches the model or the check (structural fact 2);
3. Cursor's DuneSlide: injection manipulates the parameters the model fills in, the sandbox builds its write allowlist from the manipulated values, and the overwrite lands on the sandbox executor itself [1].

One boundary of the derivation needs stating: failures without an attacker (hallucination, overstepping) are not attack surfaces; resource exhaustion is an outcome, not a path; and the user's own direct injection does not count either, because the user is the authorizing party, while third-party content the user pastes is handled as content-channel input. Attack surfaces cover only failures an attacker reaches through untrusted input.

**Attack Surface 1, context injection (S1, L2→L3, writable).** Task input, tool definitions and returns, read-in files and pages, and memory persisting across sessions converge at L2 into one unmarked token stream (structural fact 1); what writing into this surface acts on directly is the model's decisions. The injection vectors are the most ordinary content: web pages, issue comments, tool descriptions, comments inside a repository; the attacker needs no special entry point. Injection itself is delivery, not the vulnerability: how far a hijacked model can go is decided by Attack Surface 3 and Attack Surface 4, and this relation is why injection attacks always take the shape of a chain.

**Attack Surface 2, configuration (S2, CFG, writable).** Configuration files carry four classes of content: permission rules, component registration, event hooks, and runtime parameters; writing configuration changes the machine without the model and without the check. The reach paths sit at the low end of the first article's trust gradient: project-directory configuration arrives with the repository clone, and marketplace auto-installation is performed by the agent itself; both are write methods an attacker can arrange. By the count of public CVEs, this is currently the most productive attack surface.

**Attack Surface 3, permission-check bypass (S3, L4, non-interceptable).** The checking mechanism itself fails in four ways. Lexical classification can be bypassed: the mapping from command text to effect class misses semantics, and what the checker reads and what actually executes are two different things. Rules can be written or widened: allowlists written through Attack Surface 2's channels, or users induced into approving overly broad rules. Paths can be skipped wholesale: event hooks execute without the check. The review layer has blind spots: model-based review cannot see malicious intent carried in ciphertext or in execution semantics.

**Attack Surface 4, sandbox (S4, execution boundary, non-interceptable).** Sandbox failures fall into four classes. Coverage gaps: profiles usually constrain only write and execute, reads are often unlimited, and credentials stay readable. Escalation semantics: in current products, approval means running this one execution outside the sandbox, exposing the credentials in the environment along with it. Boundary escape: bypassing the boundary itself. The fourth class is boundary construction depending on model-supplied parameters: the sandbox builds its allowlist from values the model fills in, and when injection manipulates those values the boundary falls with them; DuneSlide has walked this path end to end [1].

**Attack Surface 5, persistent state (S5, L6, writable).** Once memory and session records are written into, this session's exit becomes the next session's entry: a single-session attack turns into a standing presence, and both persistence and remote control land on Attack Surface 5. The exposure of credentials consists of a read path plus a communication exit; monetization holds only when both are reachable. By the public record, this is the attack surface with the largest gap between events and research.

**Attack Surface 6, human interface (S6, L1, writable).** Presentation is a writable target, and the rendering pipeline is a channel independent of the model's decisions: in AutoGPT's CVE-2023-37275, ANSI escape sequences carried by a malicious page never touched the model; they flowed through the pipeline as data and rendered fake command output in the terminal, deceiving the person reading the screen [2]. The approval step can be deceived as well: mechanical approval under fatigue, consent requests disguised as normal flow, and interfaces that never show what will actually run.

**Attack Surface 7, framework and runtime (S7, the harness's own code, non-interceptable).** This surface points at implementation flaws in the harness's own code: serialization, checkpoints, and parsers in L2 and L5, local services, and the native runtimes themselves (V8, Electron), failing as state poisoning, parser confusion, and overwrites of trusted files. Because the object of attack is the code wrapping the model, these flaws are exploitable without the model and without the check; Langflow's unauthenticated remote code execution (CVE-2025-3248) is the canonical case: one browser visit triggers it.

Taken together, the seven attack surfaces arrange along a clear line: Attack Surface 1 and Attack Surface 2 change behavior, the first manipulating the model's decisions and the second changing the machine; Attack Surface 3 and Attack Surface 4 get past checkpoints, corresponding to the check and the sandbox; Attack Surface 5 lets an attack persist across sessions; Attack Surface 6 and Attack Surface 7 target the last lines of defense, the human and the code. No attack surface takes the model as its object; the observation at the end of the first article becomes, after the derivation, a conclusion: every checkpoint lands on the harness, the model sits outside the checkpoint sequence, and a compromised model only raises Attack Surface 1's write success rate rather than constituting an attack surface of its own.

Notably, Attack Surface 3 and Attack Surface 4 receive no direct entry: the list folds check and sandbox failures into tool misuse and unexpected code execution, while the attack-surface model sets them apart.

## The Five-Segment Structure of Attack Paths

An attack surface supplies the writable positions; turning a position into actual damage still takes a route from entry to exit, and that route is the attack path. A complete attack path has five segments, each of which draws its value from a fixed option set:

| Path segment | Option set |
|---|---|
| Channel (how it gets in) | content / service / file / internal (cross-session memory, sub-agent messages) |
| Write (which surface it lands on) | Attack Surface 1 context / Attack Surface 2 config / Attack Surface 5 state / Attack Surface 6 presentation |
| Bypass (which checkpoint lets it through) | Attack Surface 3 check / Attack Surface 4 sandbox / Attack Surface 7 framework code / human |
| Effect | read / write / execute / communicate |
| Exit | monetization / persistence / spread |

How each segment takes its value, with configuration-as-execution walked end to end: the channel is file, the malicious configuration entering with the repository clone; the write lands on Attack Surface 2, configuration; the bypass exploits the fact that event hooks never pass the check; the effect is execution; and the exit is persistence, with the written hook continuing to fire in every later session. A threat description that cannot fill all five segments does not stand, and this is the structure's most direct use.

Prototype paths are derived from the surfaces one by one, and the configuration-as-execution chain from the paragraph above is one instance: each attack surface answers the question "for an attack with this surface as home ground, what shape does each of the five segments take," and one surface's answer is one prototype path. The four writable surfaces derive write-centered paths: writing malicious content into the context (Attack Surface 1) yields the context-injection chain. The three surfaces that serve as checkpoints derive bypass-centered paths: constructing input that misleads the check (Attack Surface 3) yields the permission-bypass chain. Seven attack surfaces, seven paths; the full list:

- Context-injection chain: write malicious content into the context (Attack Surface 1), hijack the model's decisions, then pick auto-approved actions to execute;
- Configuration-as-execution chain: write rules and hooks into configuration (Attack Surface 2), trigger execution without the model or the check;
- Permission-bypass chain: construct input that misleads the check (Attack Surface 3), read sensitive files or execute commands;
- Boundary-break chain: manipulate sandbox construction parameters or exploit parsing gaps (Attack Surface 4), overwrite the executor or escape the boundary;
- State-poisoning chain: write malicious data into memory and session records (Attack Surface 5), becoming the next session's entry;
- Human-deception chain: put forged presentation on the screen (Attack Surface 6), obtain approval, and let credentials leave with the execution;
- Framework-takeover chain: exploit flaws in the framework's own code directly (Attack Surface 7), without the model or the check.

Real attacks rarely run a single prototype path. Cursor's CurXecute zero-click chain is a four-segment composite: injection delivers; the hijacked model auto-executes allowlisted actions; the chain then harvests credentials from the environment; and a reverse shell closes with persistence. Each of the four links has its own public analysis; what constitutes the threat is the complete chain that strings them together. The prototype paths are therefore not a closed taxonomy but reusable standard segments: one prototype describes one way through, and real attacks compose them as needed.

The exit segment carries one more layer of meaning. The memory, hooks, configuration, and scheduled tasks written by a persistence exit become the next session's input; the red dashed line in the diagram is exactly this loop: this session's exit connects to the next session's channel, and a one-shot attack becomes a standing presence. Inside a session runs another, smaller loop: tool results fold back into the context for the next turn of reasoning, and injection relies on it to amplify step by step. One loop stays within the session, one spans sessions, and the two stacked together are the structural reason agent attacks are hard to defend.

Articles 3 through 9 expand the seven paths in the order of the list above, each with public cases.

## Closing Notes

With this, the two pictures from the opening meet: the ten OWASP categories fit into the seven attack surfaces one by one, with no category requiring a new position. The derivation also yields the conclusion that runs through the series: all seven attack surfaces land on the harness, none takes the model as its object of attack, and a model that gets written to is only a success-rate question for Attack Surface 1, not an independent target; the five-segment structure (channel, write, bypass, effect, exit) is the shared skeleton for the path analysis in every later article.
